#### Friendship
[Bound by your history](https://www.bitsofwonder.co/p/bound-by-your-history)

> I often hold on to some kind of hope that I can actually erase my past. I want to pretend that all the years that have passed—in particular, all the difficulty and challenge, especially the challenges that didn’t have a “happy” or “palatable” ending—simply never happened. One arena in which this tendency to erase my history takes place is in friendship itself. As in: new friends give me the chance to pretend my past doesn’t exist. And to be clear, there is something relieving about this – each new moment and each new friendship really is an opportunity to begin again. It’s very exciting to connect with new people and give them the most mature, most confident form of me, without having them see my little tucked-away drawer of all the experiences and actions that I’m ashamed of. But over time I’ve found that there is a shallowness to this way of connecting with others. There is a deeper and more honest joy in connecting more deeply with the people who _have_ actually seen my ugly, messy drawer and have chosen to remain with me.

I am not sure I entirely agree with the sentiment above, but it did make me think because I have been that person who has constantly attempted to erase my past.

#### Waiting in line
[When did waiting in line become the activity?](https://www.dazeddigital.com/life-culture/article/71303/1/when-did-waiting-in-line-become-the-activity-tiktok-reels-trend-queues?utm_source=Link&utm_medium=Link&utm_campaign=RSSFeed&utm_term=when-did-waiting-in-line-become-the-activity)

> …Getting her hands on one of its elaborate flavoured iced drinks took around four hours, she says, but that long stretch of waiting has since paid off. “It was very much worth my time because the content I created at it has gotten me a lot of paid collabs and new connections with brands,” she says. “It was an inspiring and motivating way to start my time in New York.”
> 
> Once something to complain about, waiting in line for routine purchases has become a way to signal that you are part of _something_. Blame a lack of third spaces, but young people are queuing down the street for everything from [froyo](https://www.tiktok.com/@cbwritescopy/video/7672407501513526558) to [Warmies plushies](https://www.tiktok.com/@philxwinter/video/7528810383633583373) to [Rare Beauty](https://www.tiktok.com/@katarinaremovic/video/7660984327928286477) and [Tank Air pop-ups](https://www.tiktok.com/@miriacvd/video/7694817298363714847). Even if you’re not posting [“spend the day with me”](https://www.tiktok.com/@chauline_/video/7685991949228068116) videos from the queue – which many people do – you can simply walk past and [post a video](https://www.tiktok.com/@spicytunarena/video/7391614620215102762) of everyone waiting. There are even [entire dedicated accounts](https://www.instagram.com/whatisthislinefor.nyc/) asking what people in New York are queuing for, and whether it’s “line-worthy”. So, when did waiting in line become the activity?


> In an algorithmic culture, it’s easy to fall into the trap of feeling like you’ve “discovered” something new and exciting, only to show up and realise that thousands of other people were served the same video at the same time. This, naturally, shapes our experience of collective taste, where everyone is reading the same viral book, buying the same tank top and waiting for the same feed-worthy matcha, rather than stumbling into a local shop and finding something unexpected for themselves.
