#### Material Stress - The Hidden Cost of Ownership
[Material Stress, n.](https://thelivingfossils.substack.com/p/material-stress-n)

> This hiding-in-plain-sight problem is what I’m calling _material stress_: the stress created by having and managing material things.

> I am coming to realize that a substantial portion of my clients’ psychological distress can be attributed to problems like this. Much of their misery consists of thousands of small demands on their time and energy that they do not recognize as belonging to the same general category. A Ring camera alert, busted pair of shoes, expiring license, HVAC filter replacement, and dress return all seem like unrelated little annoyances. Taken together, they resemble death by a thousand cuts.


> Plus, even if we acknowledged material stress as a bona fide problem, doesn’t it have an easy solution? If you’re struggling with how much you own, just own less.
> 
> …
> 
> Owning less, however, is easier said than done.


> So why do we keep acquiring more? The simplest big-picture answer is that humans evolved in a world of scarcity and remain strongly inclined to accumulate anything that might be valuable, whether energy stored as body fat, money stored in bank accounts, or material possessions stored in our basements.
> 
> A more proximate answer is that we consumers tend to consider the price of acquiring things but not the price of having them. We evaluate a possession at precisely the moment when its benefits are most vivid and its future costs least visible. We understand what the thing will do for us now and typically factor in only the money required to acquire it; we rarely account for the rest of its life cycle, from setting it up and maintaining it to repairing and eventually replacing it.

This point about vacations is sooo trueee! 🤌🏽

> …Vacation clarifies our relationship to our possessions from the opposite direction: instead of confronting everything we own, we temporarily escape almost all of it. If moving exposes the burden, vacation reveals the relief.
> 
> I mean, doesn’t a hotel room feel so liberating in large part because there’s so little in it that we’re responsible for? If an appliance breaks, it’s not our problem. There’s no expiring cream cheese to use up, no dishes to wash or laundry to do. Our guitar isn’t hanging from the wall, silently plucking our strings of guilt. There’s no package by the door waiting to be returned. No nick in the wall we’ve been meaning for months to patch.
> 
> Vacation is usually thought of as a form of abundance: more food and drink, more leisure, more novelty. But part of what feels luxurious to me is the deprivation. For a few days, I have only a fraction of my possessions.


> Our response to material stress, to the extent we acknowledge it, is to tell ourselves that we’ll get to the important stuff once the tedium is cleared away. The problem is how much of life we spend clearing it away.
