#### Slow Life and Boredom
[Do you really want a “slow life?”](https://ktklp.substack.com/p/do-you-really-want-a-slow-life)

> Slowing down sounds admirable until you realize that most of slowness is just being bored and alone in your thoughts and rituals. Letting boredom in is transformative, yes, and being alone with your thoughts is a necessary daily practice for self-growth, indeed—but these can be uncomfortable states. Boredom was extremely uncomfortable for me when I first got rid of my phone. I felt that boredom was a failure, that my mind should either be productive all of the time, when engaged, or when not engaged, immediately enter a flow state of total zen. I started to see boredom as a gray area that I waded through as I oscillated between the two ideals.
> 
> Now boredom feels as inevitable a part of my day as sitting on the toilet or closing my eyes to fall asleep. The way to make states of boredom and loneliness less uncomfortable is to practice, and not to resist with shallow endeavors to mask them. Not photographing every angle of your morning coffee with the caption “slow mornings”[1](https://ktklp.substack.com/p/do-you-really-want-a-slow-life?utm_source=substackpost#footnote-1) or filling your spare time with scrolling—this only gives the illusion of slowness without any of the benefit.
> 
> The boredom is where you can benefit, the boredom is when your mind flourishes, blooms in interesting directions. Being alone without the panopticon of social media or your 290 unread group chat messages is when you have to really face comfort with solitude. Maybe it’s just getting older, but I feel much more comfortable being alone these days. I don’t judge myself for having nothing to do, for wasting time, for wandering about the garden slowly picking weeds or spending a half hour laying down listening to the birds outside the window. There’s no resistance in that, because any time spent being present feels like time well spent.
