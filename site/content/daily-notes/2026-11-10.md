#### Odyssey and Ramayana comparisions
[The Odyssey and the Ramayana: The Comparison That Won’t Die \| The Juggernaut](https://www.thejuggernaut.com/odyssey-ramayana-connection)

This keeps coming up, and the article does a good job of drawing out the comparisions. But at the same time, it kind of waffles around a bit and doesnt get precise about the claims around a common origin for these epics, or the exact reasons why so many aspects of the character and the plotlines appear similar.

> On the other hand, historians don’t feel confident enough to suggest that the texts are the result of cultural contact. “I think it has to be some sort of mix of both, probably some elements of diffusion and some elements of polygenesis,” Henry said, the latter referring to the independent development of storylines across geographies. “Exile, transformation, and victorious return is maybe almost by definition, a great adventure story.”

> So, yes, these texts aren’t carbon copies, nor do they try to be. So why, even after all these years, are we so obsessed with finding links between them? 
> 
> Unfortunately, or fortunately, we’re just following in the footsteps of scholars before us. In 1784, philologist William Jones [delivered](https://archive.ph/o/aW8ZF/https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1553238/9/Vasunia_Chapter%2012_A%20Handbook%20to%20the%20Reception%20of%20Classical%20Mythology.pdf) a lecture titled “On the Gods of Greece, Italy, and India.” In 1873, German Indologist Albrecht Weber [argued](https://archive.ph/o/aW8ZF/https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b5/On_the_R%C4%81m%C4%81ya%E1%B9%87a_\(IA_onramayana00webe\).pdf) that Valmiki’s _Ramayana_ had several links to Homeric texts. The bow contest, he wrote, “naturally reminds us of the bow of Odysseus.” He added, “the rape of Helen and the siege of Troy have served as a model for the corresponding incidents in the poem of Valmiki,” referring to Sita’s abduction and Lanka’s siege. “The same gods are invoked alike by Ravana and by Rama, just as is done by the Greeks and the Trojans in Homer.”
> 
> While others scholars posited that Homer was influenced by Valmiki, Weber’s hypothesis was that Valmiki was influenced by Homer. Weber believed earlier versions of the _Ramayana_ didn’t include Sita’s abduction, Ravana, and Lanka’s invasion — supporting his theory that these elements were added after hearing Homer’s epics. “I do not indeed imagine that [Valmiki] had himself studied Homer, or even that he must have been aware of the existence of the Homeric poems,” he wrote. “I content myself rather with the simple assumption that in consequence of the mutual relations, which Alexander’s expedition into India brought about…some kind of knowledge of the substance of the Homeric story found its way to India.”
> 
> In 1893, another German Indologist, Hermann Jacobi, wrote _Das Ramayana_, [countering](https://archive.ph/o/aW8ZF/https://archive.org/details/in.gov.ignca.9050) Weber’s theories. He called the _Ramayana_ “the unified work of an outstanding poet,” dating it between the 8th to 6th centuries B.C., contemporary to _The Odyssey_. For him, the “abductions” of Helen of Troy and Sita didn’t prove anything. The mechanism is also different: Sita is violently abducted, while Helen leaves consensually. “Since the abduction of women occurs almost everywhere at a certain stage of culture,” Jacobi wrote, “it is difficult to see why Indian legend could not have employed it independently as a motif.” He also pointed out that the bow was a common weapon in ancient India, so an archery contest for the hero could easily arise independently. “One searches in vain for such signs of borrowing; on the contrary, everything in the Indian narrative is fashioned differently from the Greek one.”
> 
> Which story came first, then, has been a question that has plagued us for centuries. Whom you choose to believe, Weber or Jacobi, is simply a reflection of which tradition you consider capable of ingenuity (why not both?). “That the Hindus produced such masterpieces of literature, closely rivaling Homer in beauty and in the power of portraying human emotions, is testimony to the worth and richness of the Hindu civilization,” Properzio [wrote](https://archive.ph/o/aW8ZF/https://www.jstor.org/stable/43939857?seq=1) in 2009.
