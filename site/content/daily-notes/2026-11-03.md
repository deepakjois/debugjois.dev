#### Supplements
[Against supplements](https://www.worksinprogress.news/p/against-supplements)

> My method was tripartite. I reasoned that though we can’t be absolutely sure, a supplement was, on balance, likely worth taking if three things held. First, we have a plausible evolutionary story about how we originally evolved in an environment with more of it, and our modern diets do not. Second, there is a logical, not obviously disputed, story about how it works. Third, the balance of evidence suggests that consuming the nutrient at least correlates with good health, and ideally well-designed trials show that it actually causes good health.
> 
> With such a rigorous method, how did everything fall apart? It’s a sad story. Ultimately, it was both a bad methodology and I applied it badly. The core problem is the same problem for all theorizing, the same problem the methodology of science was designed to solve: it is very easy to validate one’s tender hypotheses by looking carefully for validating evidence. Another perhaps equally big problem is that most science is nearly useless.


> But there was a more profound problem. People like the wise Stuart Ritchie and my colleague Saloni Dattani had been telling me for ages that science was overloaded with complete nonsense, which I accepted on an intellectual level but had not really absorbed into my intuitions. My feeling had always been that we could still draw conclusions if we had a big enough pile of papers. But now I have come to believe that something like [90 percent of research is nearly valueless](https://newsletter.goodscienceproject.org/p/book-review-unreliable-bias-fraud?hide_intro_popup=true).
> 
> Just as Peatism was undermining my emotional investment in supplementation, an intellectual justification for supplement scepticism appeared in the form of [a blog post by Milky Eggs](https://milkyeggs.com/biology/lifespan-extension-separating-fact-from-fiction/). The average paper in nutrition science is so bad, because of the usual range of poor research practices ranging from fraud to p-hacking, that even large bodies of research papers can have not just the wrong magnitude of effect, but the wrong sign. It is surprisingly easy to produce dozens of papers showing something false, and what’s more, there is a bandwagon effect, and people do it all the time.
