### 2026-10-30
#### Chris Arnade on reading novels
[Character Select](https://walkingtheworld.substack.com/p/character-select)

> I read novels for entertainment, but also to learn about culture because they reflect the societies that produce them, offering an ethnographic portrait of a place and time that can be hard to access by other means, especially in the distant past.
> 
> [I’m particularly interested in the archetypes they reveal](https://walkingtheworld.substack.com/p/the-hero-and-other-cultural-characters?r=21uj1) — the array of roles a society makes available, the models of personhood we can inhabit. Think of character selection in a video game.
> 
> These characters change from place to place and through time — novels both portray them and can change them. National stereotypes are no longer in vogue (deservedly so when they’re used to judge individuals), but they are largely correct. They are folk ethnography, a shorthand summary of the most popular choices on a culture’s character-select screen. The Japanese salaryman, the entrepreneurial American, and the English pub-dwellers are caricatures, but they are caricatures of the most popular roles available.
> 
> I’ve written several times before about my belief that every culture does this, because it’s one of the most consequential ways thick culture[1](https://walkingtheworld.substack.com/p/character-select#footnote-1) shapes life. Humans are cultural creatures, imbued with the ability to live any type of life, but only choosing to live one. Unlike any other animal, we hold the potential for many different selves, yet we settle into just one, and that self is a manifestation of where and when we grow up.

> Yet, there are plenty of differences beyond appearance, embedded into the core of who we are, and how we see ourselves — our work ethic, concepts of honor, relationship to authority, attitude toward family obligations, definition of a successful life, conception of good and evil, and our understanding of beauty, art, and leisure.
> 
> For several decades it became intellectually fashionable to try and find human cultural universals, but in the end anthropologists found none. Or none that weren’t, as [Clifford Geertz](https://walkingtheworld.substack.com/p/thick-travel) noted, either too trivial (we all need food) or too abstract (we all have a sense of justice) to divine much of anything about the human condition.
> 
> That doesn’t mean any group is not human, or without a soul (I am a Catholic who believes in universal human dignity after all), but that becoming one big happy global family isn’t an easy task, certainly not one that’s a shared Coke away.
> 
> Any traveler knows this, especially modern travelers, because while globalization has rendered our physical landscapes similar, you still feel like a stranger in a strange land. When in a foreign culture you are still an alien, and with that comes all sorts of misunderstandings, confusions, and imbalances[3](https://walkingtheworld.substack.com/p/character-select#footnote-3).
