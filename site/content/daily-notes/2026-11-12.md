### 2026-11-12
#### Technological Progress
[Pretty Botoxic](https://thelivingfossils.substack.com/p/pretty-botoxic)

> Ideally, we’d evaluate technologies by whether they produce net long-term improvements in human well-being. But even if we could answer that extraordinarily difficult question, we’d face another problem: controlling their adoption. Technologies often spread less through collective deliberation than through competitive imitation. People adopt them because they confer advantages, but once enough people do, abstaining can become a disadvantage (looking at you, smartphones).
> 
> This makes evolutionary sense. Natural selection didn’t design us to maximize happiness; it shaped us to pursue outcomes that promoted reproductive success. Technology is one means to those ends. Our experiences matter, from evolution’s perspective, chiefly insofar as they steer adaptive behavior. It is behavior that leaves traces in the evolutionary record through its effect on genes; our experience disappears like soft tissue.
> 
> The Botox arms race, then, can produce a social dilemma: everyone might be better off abstaining, yet each person may be better off participating once others do. Escaping such dilemmas requires coordination. As [Rob has argued](https://thelivingfossils.substack.com/p/athletes-play-coordination-games), shared rules can “eliminate incentives to make individually advantageous but collectively undesirable choices.” Bans and taxes are two ways of attempting to align those incentives.
> 
> But coordination needn’t come from government. Moral norms and cultural institutions, including religion, can also help align individual and collective interests. Then again, culture can just as easily encourage the competition it might otherwise restrain. Consider some familiar American imperatives: _Work hard and get ahead. Improve yourself. Don’t settle. Seize new opportunities. Make your own choices._ Excellent advice for individual success; less useful when the race itself is the problem.

Ending with a succinct but profound thought.

> And so we keep running—not always because we’re going somewhere, but often because, in the absence of the group figuring out how to stop, we as individuals can’t afford to.
